## 1. The reframe that matters most

The instinct is to pick a country and apply. That is the wrong shape for this problem.

With a Haitian passport, applying from the US, against a hard date, **every visa-required route is a single point of failure.** A consulate that won't book a third-country national, a request for extra documents in November, one refusal in December — and you are at 20 January with nothing. The US State Department tightened third-country-national processing in September 2025, and while that rule governs *US* visas, the underlying pattern (consulates preferring applicants in their country of nationality or lawful residence) is common. Your leverage is that you hold valid US status *right now*, and that ends.

So build the plan around routes that need **zero consular approval** — where you buy a ticket and board — and run the visa-required routes in parallel as upside only.

That is achievable. Your passport opens more doors on arrival than its reputation suggests.

**Two things to start this week, in parallel:**

1. **File the Benin Afro-descendant nationality application.** $100, ~6–12 weeks, and the payoff is a passport rather than a visa. Details in §3.
2. **Fix a no-visa bridge destination and hold a ticket.** This is your floor. It guarantees you leave the US legally on your own terms regardless of how anything else goes.

## 3. The Benin nationality route — highest ceiling, start this week

This is the finding that changes the shape of the plan, because it produces a **nationality**, not a permission slip.

**Law 2024-31 of 2 September 2024**, amended by **Law 2025-11 of 1 July 2025**, grants Beninese nationality to Afro-descendants — people whose genealogy includes a sub-Saharan African ancestor deported during the transatlantic slave trade. The application platform, **My Afro Origins** (`myafroorigins.bj`), launched 4 July 2025 and operates in French, English, Portuguese and Spanish.

**Why you are close to an ideal applicant:** the requirement is to trace ancestry to a person of African descent **born before 1944** in a recognised territory, and to not already hold a sub-Saharan African nationality. You are Haitian-born; your parents and grandparents are Haitian-born. The lineage that a US-born applicant has to reconstruct across a documentary gap is, for you, your own family's civil records. **A Haitian national, Gabendy Joseph, was among the first beneficiaries.**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Fee** | $100 (+ ~$4 processing) |
| **Realistic total cost** | ~$265 including background check and vital records |
| **Processing** | Up to 3 months by regulation; one documented case came back in **~6 weeks** |
| **Evidence** | Certified civil records (birth/death/marriage), valid ID, fingerprint criminal background check, proof of residence, proof of employment, plus a **~500-word ancestry narrative**. You do **not** have to prove direct lineage to a specific named enslaved person |
| **DNA test** | **Optional**, and cannot establish Afro-descent on its own. The documented approved applicant submitted none. Don't let this become a delay |
| **Language** | Supporting documents must be translated into French |
| **Outcome** | An eligibility certificate valid 3 years → **provisional nationality**, including the right to a **Beninese passport and residency**. Not yet voting rights or public office |
| **Final step** | You must **travel to Benin in person** at least once to convert provisional to full citizenship |

**The in-person requirement is not a problem for you — it is the plan.** Haitians enter Benin visa-free for 90 days. You fly to Cotonou before 20 January and complete it on the ground.

### What a Beninese passport is actually worth

More than its own visa-free count. Benin is an ECOWAS member, and the ECOWAS Protocol on Free Movement of Persons, Residence and Establishment covers **15 countries**: Benin, Burkina Faso, Cabo Verde, Côte d'Ivoire, Gambia, Ghana, Guinea, Guinea-Bissau, Liberia, Mali, Niger, Nigeria, Senegal, Sierra Leone, Togo.

Phase one (visa-free entry) and phase two (**right of residence**, via an ECOWAS residence card) are implemented. Phase three (right of establishment — setting up a business or profession on equal footing with nationals) is **still only partially implemented** across member states, so don't assume it.

Net: a permanent legal right to enter and reside across a market of roughly 400 million people, with no visa clock — replacing a passport that currently gets you 90 days at a time in about a dozen places.

### The honest risks

- **The pre-1944 ancestor documentation is the real hurdle.** You need Haitian civil records reaching back to a great-grandparent's generation. Obtaining *actes de naissance* from the Archives Nationales d'Haïti under current conditions is the part most likely to slow you down. Start it first, this week, before anything else — likely through a relative or a *fondé de pouvoir* in Haiti.
- **The programme is new and discretionary.** It launched thirteen months ago. There is no long track record of refusal rates or appeal routes, and it has drawn criticism as reputational politics. It is a strong bet, not a guarantee.
- **Provisional ≠ full.** Three years, conditional on the in-person visit.

**Therefore: file it now, and still hold a bridge ticket.** These are not alternatives to each other.

## 4. Routes worth running in parallel — and one to write off

**Colombia — Digital Nomad Visa (Type V).** Fully online through the Cancillería portal, no interview, **2–6 weeks**. Requires income of at least 3× Colombian minimum wage — **~$1,435/month in 2026** — evidenced by personal bank statements for the three months before you apply, and **each month must individually clear the threshold; they are not averaged.** Grants up to two years. Income must be foreign-sourced; it does not authorise work for Colombian employers. Medellín runs **$1,200–3,000/month**. This is the single best home for the prop-trading income *if* it materialises on schedule — but note the three-month statement requirement means your banking has to be clean and above threshold starting around **September** to file in December.

**Brazil — VITEM XIV digital nomad visa.** Filed at a Brazilian consulate in the US; the published bar is around $1,500/month income or $18,000 in savings. Portuguese, low cost of living, and the largest Haitian community in the hemisphere outside North America.

**Brazil's humanitarian visa for Haitians — currently closed.** Worth stating clearly because it is the route people will tell you to take. An interministerial ordinance effective **1 January 2026** revoked the country-specific humanitarian schemes, including the Haitian one, and replaced them with a single framework whose eligible-nationality list must be set by a future joint ministerial act that **has not been issued**. Consulates have been instructed **not to presume eligibility for any group** — a standing pause on new filings. The grandfather clause covers only those already in Brazil on or before 31 December 2025. Do not build a plan on this reopening before January.

**Canada — study permit. Low probability; treat as lottery ticket, not plan.** The appeal is obvious: French-language Quebec, the largest Haitian diaspora anywhere, and applications filed from the US process in about **4–6 weeks** versus 8–16 elsewhere. But: SDS was discontinued in November 2024, so you are in the regular stream; you need a Designated Learning Institution acceptance plus a Provincial Attestation Letter (Quebec PALs run 6–8 weeks in peak season) plus a CAQ; you need roughly **CAD 20,635** in settlement funds on top of tuition of CAD 15,000–25,000; and the officer must be satisfied you will leave at the end. An applicant whose US status is expiring, applying from the US, has a structurally hard time on that last test. If you want to try it, run it alongside — never instead of — the no-visa bridge.

## 5. Money: the trading plan, and what it can and can't buy

Your plan: **25–40 funded accounts × $50/day, over 1–2 months.** At ~22 trading days per month that is **$27,500 to $88,000** — then used as declared funds.

I'll take the plan as given and flag what will actually bite:

1. **Per-firm capital caps force you across multiple firms.** Firms cap total funded capital per trader, typically **$300,000–$600,000** (FTMO is $400,000), and generally allow **3–20 accounts each**. Twenty-five to forty accounts means spreading across roughly **4–8 different firms**. Across firms there is effectively no restriction — Firm A has no visibility into Firm B — but you are now managing 4–8 rulebooks, KYC processes and payout cycles simultaneously.
2. **Every funded account starts as a paid challenge.** This is the cost the headline number hides. Getting to 25–40 *funded* accounts means buying substantially more challenges than that. Budget it as real, upfront, non-refundable spend.
3. **Payout lag eats your last month.** First payouts typically land **14–30 days** after funding, then on a cycle. Money earned in November may not clear a bank account until December. Against a 20 January wall, **assume early January is your last usable payout** and work backwards.
4. **Correlation rules apply within a firm.** Never hold opposite or economically correlated positions across accounts at the same firm — many treat ES/NQ/MES as one exposure. Copying your own strategy across your own accounts is permitted at virtually every major firm; using someone else's signals to pass a challenge is not.

### Using it as proof of funds — the part that gets people refused

The specific failure mode is documented and consular officers are trained on it: **"window dressing."** A large sum appearing shortly before an application, with no history behind it, reads as borrowed money and produces a refusal rather than an approval. A stack of payments from offshore prop firms landing in November–December, followed by a visa application in December, is close to a textbook example.

Mitigations, in order of importance:

- **Start banking payouts as early as you can and let the balance season.** Three to six months of history is what turns a number into a pattern. This alone is a reason to start the trading push in August rather than October.
- **Keep a documentation pack per firm:** the trader agreement, account statements, payout confirmations or invoices, and the matching bank credit. Unexplained deposits must each be traceable to a document.
- **Receive payouts into a bank account in your own name** wherever the firm allows, rather than crypto wallets or third-party processors. Officers read bank statements; they do not read wallets.
- **Consistency beats a single big month** — Colombia's DNV literally requires each of three months to clear the threshold independently.

### The strategic point

**Weight the plan toward routes that do not test funds at all.** Benin nationality costs $100 and asks about your ancestry, not your balance. Rwanda's entry costs nothing. Senegal's VOA costs nothing. The Philippines asks for an onward ticket.

Under that weighting the trading income stops being a *gate* you must clear by December and becomes **runway** — 6 to 24 months of living costs at Cotonou or Kigali prices, which is exactly what you want it to be. If it comes in at $30,000 instead of $88,000, or two months late, the plan still works. That is the whole point of sequencing it this way.

## 6. Two logistics traps

**Never book a ticket that transits Europe.** Haitian ordinary passport holders require a **Schengen Type A airport transit visa** — France explicitly requires it, and Spain has required it for Haitian ordinary passports since 1 September 2011. This applies to sitting in the international transit area without ever entering the country. A cheap fare through Paris, Lisbon, Amsterdam, Madrid, Brussels or Frankfurt is not a cheap fare; it is a denied boarding.

Route through **Istanbul** (Turkish), **Doha** (Qatar), **Addis Ababa** (Ethiopian), **Casablanca** (Royal Air Maroc), **Nairobi** (Kenya Airways) or **Dubai** instead. Confirmed connections: Ethiopian serves **Washington Dulles → Addis Ababa → Cotonou**; Qatar and RwandAir both serve **Doha → Kigali**; Turkish flies Istanbul–Kigali seasonally. Best of all, **Delta's nonstop JFK/Atlanta → Dakar** involves no transit country whatsoever.

**Confirm transit rules with the airline for your specific hub before paying.** I verified the Schengen requirement; I did not verify the Gulf and African hubs individually.

**Yellow fever vaccination.** Benin, Senegal and the West/Central African region generally require a yellow fever certificate for entry, and Rwanda requires one if you arrive from an endemic country. The certificate is only valid from **10 days after** vaccination. Get this done in September, not in January — it is a trivial thing that can stop you at a gate.

## 9. Philippines — routing and entry (selected route)

### Fly direct from the US. This is the whole answer.

**Philippine Airlines operates nonstop US–Manila service from New York (JFK), Los Angeles, San Francisco, Seattle and Honolulu**, adding **Chicago O'Hare from 9 November 2026** (3× weekly, Mon/Wed/Fri, already on sale).

A nonstop means **no transit country at all**. Every transit-visa question in §6 — Schengen Type A, Gulf hubs, Japan, Korea — disappears. For a Haitian passport holder this is worth more than any fare saving: the most common failure mode is being denied boarding over a transit rule, and a nonstop makes that structurally impossible.

**Book the nonstop even if it costs several hundred dollars more.** It is the cheapest insurance on this page.

One-stop alternatives if the nonstop doesn't work on price or date: **Turkish TK84 Istanbul–Manila** (nonstop, 4× weekly, 787, ~11h15m) is the best of them — and you're eVisa-eligible for Turkey (90 days in 180) anyway, so an Istanbul connection carries no documentary risk even if you have to clear immigration. Qatar via Doha, Emirates via Dubai and Etihad via Abu Dhabi also serve Manila; confirm their transit rules individually before booking.

### Entry requirements — the one that gets people stopped

| Requirement | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Passport validity** | 6+ months beyond stay. Yours runs to 2034 ✓ |
| **Onward/return ticket** | **Mandatory, and enforced at check-in — not at the border.** Airlines are required to verify a confirmed exit plan before issuing a boarding pass |
| **eTravel registration** | Free, at `etravel.gov.ph`, within **72 hours** before landing. Replaced paper arrival cards in 2026 |
| **Proof of funds** | Not always checked; be ready to show ~$50/day for your stay via bank statement, card or cash |
| **Stay granted** | 30 days on arrival |

**The onward ticket is the thing to get right.** You are flying one-way into a 36-month plan, and the check-in agent in New York will not board you without a ticket out of the Philippines dated within your 30-day entry. Buy a cheap onward flight to Kuala Lumpur, Hong Kong or Singapore before you go to the airport. It is a one-time problem — it applies at boarding, not at each subsequent extension.

### The extension ladder

30 days on arrival → **29-day visa waiver extension** (to 59 days) → rolling **9(a) extensions** in one-, two- or six-month blocks → **36-month cumulative cap** for visa-exempt nationals. Convertible in-country to a **9F student visa**; note that tourist time already used counts against the 36-month cap.

### What the Philippines is and isn't

It is the best **bridge** on the list: no visa, English, ~$900–1,500/month in Cebu, cheap English-taught universities, and three years of legal runway bought one extension at a time.

It is not a **destination**: 9(a) status carries no work rights, and there is no path from it to permanent residence or citizenship. **Keep the Benin application (§3) running in parallel.** The sequence that uses both: land in Manila before 20 January, extend while the nationality decision processes, then fly Manila → Cotonou (via Istanbul, Doha or Addis) to claim it in person inside the three-year certificate window.

### Do not route through Haiti — there is effectively no path

Direct answer to the question: **you cannot sensibly fly Haiti → Philippines, and you should not try.**

- **Port-au-Prince (PAP) is closed to US carriers.** The FAA ban has been extended repeatedly — currently through **7 March 2026** — because armed groups threaten civil aviation. Gangs control roughly 90% of the capital.
- **Cap-Haïtien (CAP) is now the country's main international gateway**, but it is served by only five airlines — American, Bahamasair, Caicos Express, interCaribbean and Sunrise — flying short-haul only: Miami, Fort Lauderdale, Boston, Newark, Kingston, Turks & Caicos, Nassau, plus Santo Domingo from October 2026. **Nothing from CAP connects toward Asia.**
- **Every usable onward hub is blocked or gated.** The US options need a US visa you will not hold once you have departed. The non-US options — Santo Domingo, Panama, Kingston — require a Haitian-passport visa each.
- The only chain that theoretically works is something like **CAP → Santo Domingo or Panama → Bogotá/Panama → Istanbul (Turkish TK800, 3× weekly) → Manila (TK84)**. That is multiple visas, several days, and three separate points where you can be denied boarding.

**The practical conclusion:** the nonstop from New York or Los Angeles is an asset that **expires with your US status**. It exists now and will not exist after 20 January. Fly out of the US directly to Manila — do not build any plan that involves touching Haiti first.

## 10. Digital nomad visas — the right question, with one structural catch

DNVs are unusually attractive for you because **almost all of them test income or assets, not passport**. Malaysia's DE Rantau is open to every nationality except Israel; Thailand's DTV is open to all nationalities at 20+. For a passport that gets refused on nationality elsewhere, that is a rare piece of level ground.

The catch is not your passport. **It's that prop trading doesn't look like remote work.**

### The structural problem

Most DNVs don't test "do you have money." They test "do you have a remote *job*." Thailand's workcation category wants an employment contract, or client contracts plus invoices, or business registration with revenue proof. Colombia requires foreign-sourced income and does not authorise local work. Mauritius says you may only work for clients abroad. Brazil wants income "from foreign employers, clients, or your own company registered abroad."

A prop trader has **no employer, no clients, and no invoices.** Payouts are a profit share on a funded account, not a fee for services. A consular officer reading that file sees savings, not employment — and the documented rejection pattern is explicit: *"generic portfolio or vague freelance contracts get rejected."*

So the play is to target the visas that test **assets**, and to sidestep the ones that test employment.

### What's actually open to you

**The Philippines' own DNV won't help you.** Executive Order 86 requires applicants to come from a country that offers a **reciprocal digital nomad visa to Filipino nationals**. Haiti has no DNV, and no official qualifying-country list had been published as of March 2026. Your Philippines plan stays on the 9(a) extension ladder from §9 — which is fine, because that ladder has no funds test at all.

**Cape Verde is worth remembering.** It's restricted to citizens of Europe, North America, the CPLP and **ECOWAS**. Closed to you today — but Benin is an ECOWAS member, so a Beninese passport (§3) opens it. Small thing, but it shows how the nationality play compounds.

### The Thailand DTV Soft Power route — the one to actually chase

This is the finding worth acting on. The DTV has two qualifying doors, and everyone talks about the wrong one.

**Soft Power** requires only:
1. **500,000 THB (~$14,500)** in your own personal account, seasoned 3 months, and
2. **Enrollment in a qualifying cultural programme** — Muay Thai at a government-recognised gym, or an accredited Thai cooking school.

That's it. **No employment contract, no clients, no invoices** — the enrollment *is* your qualifying activity. For someone with capital and no employer, this door was built to fit.

Practical notes: the gym or school must be officially recognised, not any gym. Programmes of **6+ months** approve far better; 9–12 months is recommended. You need an acceptance letter, a formal training schedule, and payment receipts — not drop-in classes. **Thai language study does not qualify** — embassies have been instructed to reject it, since it belongs to the ED visa.

Outcome: a **5-year multiple-entry visa**, 180 days per entry, ~$400 fee applying from the US. Compare that to 30 days at a time in Manila.

### The scheduling problem — read this before doing anything

Every asset-tested DNV has the same requirement, and it is the same one from §5: **the money has to have been sitting still.**

Thai embassies scrutinise 3–6 months of transactions specifically for the "parking money" pattern — a large deposit appearing shortly before you apply. One practitioner estimate puts **~70% of DIY DTV rejections down to bank statement problems**: parked money, wrong currency, insufficient history. Colombia requires each of three months to clear the bar independently.

Now do the arithmetic against your timeline. If the trading push starts in August and payouts land September–November, the three-month seasoning clock doesn't finish until **December at the earliest, more likely February**. That is at or past your 20 January wall.

**So: a DNV is not a January visa for you. It is a month-6 visa.** Attempting to force one before departure is how you end up with a rejection on file and no ticket.

### How to sequence it

**Floor (unchanged):** fly nonstop to Manila before 20 January on the 9(a) ladder. No funds test, no seasoning requirement, no consular discretion. This is why the Philippines was the right pick.

**Then, one of two branches:**

- **If the money lands early and seasons by November** — apply for the Thailand DTV from the US in November or December, while you still hold US status and a US consular jurisdiction that will accept you. Best case: you fly to Bangkok on a five-year visa instead of to Manila on thirty days.
- **Otherwise (the likely case)** — go to Manila, let the balance sit untouched for three to six months while you extend, book a Muay Thai or cooking programme in Thailand, and apply for the DTV from Manila around month four to six. The DTV must be applied for from outside Thailand, and the Philippines qualifies.

⚠️ **One caveat on that second branch:** Thai embassies prioritise applicants resident in their jurisdiction and may ask for proof of legal residence — a residence permit or long-term visa. A Philippine tourist extension may not satisfy that. Confirm with the Royal Thai Embassy in Manila **before** you rely on it; if they decline third-country applicants, the fallback is applying to a post that does, or using Brazil's VITEM XIV instead.

**The through-line across §5, §9 and §10: don't touch the money.** Whatever the trading produces, park it in one personal account and leave it alone. Every asset-tested route you might want — Thailand, Brazil — is unlocked by an untouched balance with history, and destroyed by moving it around.

## 11. Australia and Japan

Neither is a January exit route — both need lead time you no longer have. But **Japan is a genuinely strong year-one target**, and it's reachable *from Manila*. Australia is the weakest option on this page and I'd drop it.

### Japan — the best long-term destination assessed so far

Japan is the only high-income country in this entire document with a **work visa that does not require a degree**.

**The Specified Skilled Worker (SSW) visa** covers 16 labour-shortage sectors — nursing care, agriculture, construction, manufacturing, hospitality — with **no degree and no prior training in Japan required**. You need a sector skills test plus **JLPT N4 or JFT-Basic A2**. That is the whole gate. For someone with a secondary education and a technical certificate, this is the most accessible route into a wealthy economy that exists.

Structure matters here:
- **SSW Type 1** — 5 years cumulative, no family, requires a registered support organisation, and **time on Type 1 does not count toward permanent residence.**
- **SSW Type 2** — expanded since August 2023 to nearly all sectors except care work. **Indefinitely renewable, family allowed, and it does count toward PR.**

⚠️ **New CoEs for SSW Type 1 in Food Service were suspended from 13 April 2026** — the sector's 50,000-worker cap for FY2024–2028 was projected to be hit by May 2026. Check current sector caps before committing to a track; caps close without much warning.

**The usual on-ramp is a Japanese language school**, and the numbers are far friendlier than Australia's:

| | |
|---|---|
| **Funds required** | ¥2,000,000–2,500,000 for a one-year programme (~$13,000–17,000); ~¥1,500,000 for six months |
| **Education needed** | 12 years of schooling — **you have this** |
| **Work rights** | Part-time work permitted with the standard permission endorsement |
| **COE processing** | 2–3 months, issued ~1 month before arrival |
| **Apply** | 6–8 months ahead of intake |

⚠️ **New as of 15 April 2026 — and this one is a hard gate.** Student visa applicants entering Japanese language institutions must now demonstrate **A1-level Japanese or above** (≈ **JLPT N5**). Accepted evidence: JLPT, NAT-TEST, J.TEST, BJT or another Immigration Services Agency–approved exam — **or an exam or formal interview conducted by a teacher at the language school itself.** That last option is the practical escape hatch if you can't get a test sitting in time.

Note also that Japan scrutinises **source of funds** hard — immigration wants "a coherent story: salary deposits over time," with third-party sponsors needing tax certificates and proof of relationship. Same seasoning discipline as §5 and §10.

**The timeline, and why it works from Manila:**

April 2027 intake deadlines are **mid-to-late October 2026** — Nihongo Center closes 31 October, KOKO Japan 15 October. That's roughly ten weeks from now.

So the sequence is: **apply to a Japanese language school in September–October 2026 from the US → fly nonstop to Manila before 20 January → COE issued around February → collect the visa at the Japanese Embassy in Manila → enter Japan April 2027.** The three-month gap between your US departure and the Japanese intake is exactly what the Manila 9(a) ladder is for. This is the strongest use of the bridge I've found.

### Australia — I'd drop it

| Requirement | Reality |
|---|---|
| Living-cost funds | **AUD 29,710** (up from 24,505), on top of tuition and OSHC — realistically **AUD 45,000–55,000** demonstrable |
| English | IELTS **6.0 overall, no band below 5.5** — and **online/at-home test variants are explicitly not accepted** |
| Genuine Student test | Replaced GTE in March 2024, assessed inside the application |
| Evidence Level | High-risk profiles get **manual verification of everything** — 3–6 months of bank history, authenticated transcripts, verification calls to providers, discretionary interviews |
| Payoff | Post-study 485 for a diploma/trade qualification is **18 months only**, under-35, fee AUD 5,750 |

Australia moved India, Nepal, Bangladesh and Bhutan to Evidence Level 3 in January 2026 citing fraudulent financial documents and falsified transcripts. Haiti's assigned level isn't published, but nothing about the profile suggests lenient treatment.

Put together: roughly **triple Japan's funds bar**, an in-person English test you don't yet have, heavy documentary scrutiny of exactly the money that will look freshly deposited — and eighteen months of work rights at the end of it. **The effort is better spent on Japanese.**

## 12. Which online credentials are actually recognised

You asked this earlier and I answered it for the wrong set of destinations. For Benin, Rwanda and Manila, "skip it" was right — none of them gate on credentials. **With Japan in scope that changes: one credential is now a hard entry requirement, not a booster.** Here is the real answer.

**The rule that explains everything below: immigration authorities recognise *accredited qualifications* and *standardised tests*. They do not recognise course-completion certificates.** Udemy is not accredited by any regional, national or international body, and its certificates cannot be used for formal accreditation. The same is true of Coursera and edX certificates of completion, as distinct from actual accredited degrees delivered through those platforms.

### Tier 1 — recognised by immigration. These unlock visas.

| Credential | Unlocks | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **JLPT N5** (or NAT-TEST / J.TEST) | **Japanese language school entry** | **Mandatory since 15 April 2026.** JLPT runs only July and December — **NAT-TEST and J.TEST run far more often**, which matters against an October deadline |
| **JLPT N4 or JFT-Basic A2** | **Japan SSW work visa** | JFT-Basic is offered frequently at registered centres, unlike JLPT's twice-yearly schedule |
| **SSW sector skills test** | Japan SSW work visa | No degree required |
| **IELTS / PTE / TOEFL iBT** | Australia, Canada, UK | **Must be sat in person** — at-home variants rejected. Study online, test in a centre |

### Tier 2 — recognised by employers. These get you hired, not admitted.

CompTIA A+ / Network+ / Security+, AWS Cloud Practitioner and Solutions Architect Associate, Cisco CCNA, Microsoft certifications. All online-proctored, globally portable, roughly **$150–400** each, and genuinely respected by hiring managers. Google Career Certificates (IT Support, Data Analytics, Project Management, UX) sit just below these.

None of them will ever appear on a visa checklist. They earn income once you have status — which is a real thing, just not the thing you asked about.

### Tier 3 — counts for nothing official

Udemy, Coursera and edX completion certificates, LinkedIn Learning. Portfolio signals only. **No immigration authority anywhere counts these toward education requirements, skill levels or points.**

### The distinction that matters: accredited institution vs. platform certificate

It is **not** true that online study can't be accredited. Plenty of it is. The line is not online-vs-in-person — it is **who issues the credential**:

- An **accredited institution** that happens to teach online grants a real degree. It is evaluated and it counts.
- A **platform** issues a certificate of completion. It is not a qualification and never becomes one.

Concrete example: **University of the People** — fully online and tuition-free apart from assessment fees — was awarded **WSCUC accreditation in February 2025**. WSCUC is US *regional* accreditation, the highest tier, the same body that accredits Berkeley, UCLA and Stanford. It voluntarily ended its older DEAC (national) accreditation on 31 December 2025, which is an upgrade, not a loss.

Degrees from recognised institutions are evaluated for immigration by bodies like **WES**, which is accepted by IRCC in Canada and USCIS in the US — roughly **$186–264**, about 7 days.

So an accredited online bachelor's is real, portable and cheap. It is simply **too slow for this window** — three to four years. That makes it a **year 1–4 project to run from Manila or Japan**, in parallel with everything else, not a January tool. Worth starting once you have landed and the rent is $300.

### What to actually do between now and January

**Study Japanese to N5 and sit NAT-TEST or J.TEST.** It is the single highest-return thing available to you online, for four reasons: it's cheap; it is a **hard legal gate** rather than a nice-to-have; at N4 the same study track opens the **SSW work visa** — the only realistic non-degree work route into a high-income country in this document; and the Japan Foundation's official **Irodori** and **Marugoto** courses are free online, from the same body that administers JFT-Basic.

Note the deadline interaction: **JLPT's December 2026 sitting returns results too late for an October school deadline.** Use NAT-TEST or J.TEST, or lean on the school's own teacher-conducted interview option.

If you want a second thing, add **one** vendor certification (CompTIA A+ or AWS Cloud Practitioner) for employability. Do not stack MOOC certificates — they will not move any decision that matters here.
